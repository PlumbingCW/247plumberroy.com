Urgent first

Active leaks and hidden leaks both need quick answers

If you can see water, shut off the main and call. If you cannot see water but your bill jumped, the meter spins with every fixture off, or a floor feels warm or damp, the leak is hidden and every day it runs costs money and risks mold and structural damage.

Our 24/7 line takes both kinds of call. A visible gush is a burst pipe emergency, while a hidden leak is often found in a scheduled visit with listening and imaging gear.

Do it yourself first

A two-minute test to see if you have a leak

Before you call, you can confirm whether the leak is real and which side of the meter it is on.

  1. 1

    Turn off every fixture.

    Close faucets, stop the dishwasher and washer, and make sure the ice maker and any irrigation are not running.

  2. 2

    Read the meter.

    Find the meter, usually near the street or in a box by the property line. Note the reading or watch the small leak dial.

  3. 3

    Wait fifteen minutes.

    Do not use water. Check again. If the dial moved, water is moving somewhere.

  4. 4

    Close the main and recheck.

    If the dial stops after you close the main shutoff in the house, the leak is inside. If it keeps moving, it is on the line between the meter and the house.

The city generally maintains the line up to its meter. The line from the meter into your house is typically the homeowner's responsibility. Call us if you are unsure.

How we find leaks

What a plumber uses to trace a hidden leak

Finding a leak without tearing out walls is the point of leak detection.

Acoustic listening.

Sensitive microphones pick up the sound of water escaping from pressurized pipe.

Thermal imaging.

A thermal camera shows the temperature difference where hot lines leak into slab or wall cavities.

Pressure testing.

Isolating sections of line and watching pressure helps narrow a leak to one zone.

Moisture meters.

Readings on drywall, subfloor and baseboards show where water has traveled.

No single tool finds everything. The plumber chooses the combination that matches your symptom, then confirms before opening anything.

Local conditions

Why Roy homes see pinhole and fitting leaks

Roy has a broad mix of homes from the 1940s to today. Older galvanized and early copper lines can develop pinholes as the inside corrodes, and some late 1970s to mid 1990s homes carry polybutylene supply pipe that is prone to failing at fittings. Matching the pipe type to the symptom is half of finding the leak.

The city's water plan reports delivery pressure of 60 to 90 psi. That is a comfortable range for taps but a heavy load on a worn line. If pressure is on the high end, a regulator reduces stress on pipes and fixtures, and it is worth checking even after a leak is fixed.

Prevention

Habits that reduce leak damage

You cannot stop every leak, but you can limit what one does.

  • Find your main shutoff now and test that it turns.
  • Check under sinks and behind toilets twice a year.
  • Replace rubber washing machine hoses before they fail.
  • Look at the water heater base each month.
  • Watch your meter dial after a long trip or vacation.
